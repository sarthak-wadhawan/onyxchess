Piece movement

Before asking whether a move is legal, we need to know where a piece could go according to its movement pattern. We call these candidates pseudo-legal moves. King safety is checked separately in the next lesson.

The following boards isolate movement geometry. They intentionally omit full game state so that we can study one rule at a time.

Knights: fixed offsets

A knight jumps two squares along one axis and one along the other. There are eight possible combinations. Because it jumps, the pieces between its current square and destination do not matter.

How add_knight_moves works

  1. Scan the board for knights belonging to the current player.
  2. For each knight, add each of the eight offsets to its rank and file.
  3. Discard any destination outside the board or occupied by a friendly piece.
  4. Record every remaining destination and the captured piece, if any.

Move the knight from d4 toward an edge. Fewer offsets remain within the board; the movement algorithm itself does not change.

Sliding pieces: follow a direction

A bishop moves along four diagonal rays. A ray is a sequence of squares in one direction. Start one step away from the piece and keep moving in that direction until a boundary or blocker is reached.

How add_sliding_moves works

  • Empty square: add a move and continue the ray.
  • Friendly piece: stop without adding a move.
  • Opposing piece: add a capture and stop.
  • Outside the board: stop.

The bishop on d4 cannot land on b2 or pass the pawn there. It can capture the rook on f6, but cannot move beyond it on that ray.

Rooks

The rook uses the same function with four straight directions instead of diagonals. Each step changes either rank or file, not both.

From d4, the knight on d6 can be captured. Squares d7 and d8 cannot be reached in the same move. The friendly pawn at b4 blocks the opposite ray without being a valid destination.

Queens

A queen combines the bishop's four diagonal directions and the rook's four straight directions. We do not need a third algorithm: pass all eight directions into the same sliding function.

Try the queen at d4. The same rules for empty squares, friendly blockers and captures apply on every ray. This is a practical example of reusing logic instead of duplicating it.

Pawns: movement depends on context

A pawn moves differently depending on color, rank and occupancy. White moves toward increasing ranks; Black moves toward decreasing ranks.

How add_pawn_moves works

  1. Look one square forward. Add a push only when it is empty.
  2. If the pawn is on its starting rank and both forward squares are empty, also add the two-square push.
  3. Inspect the two forward diagonals. A normal capture requires an opposing piece.
  4. Recognize promotion and en passant separately; these require extra state or move information.

The pawn on e2 may advance to e3 or e4, or capture the knight on d3. The friendly pawn on f3 cannot be captured. A pawn attacks diagonally even if those squares are empty.

Kings: one step and a safety rule

The add_king_moves helper tries the eight neighboring squares. It discards off-board destinations and friendly pieces. Unlike a queen, the king never continues along the direction. Castling is a separate two-square action.

A geometric move is not necessarily safe: a king cannot move onto a defended square. This example displays geometry only. In a complete position, legal move filtering rejects unsafe king moves.

Collect the candidates

The generate_pseudo_legal_moves function calls the movement helpers for the active color and gathers their results in one list. Each move records its source, destination and capture details. The sliding helper is called three times with different direction sets.

The next lesson takes this list and removes moves that would expose the king.
